Chapter~2 established that researchers do not experience literature work as a sequence of isolated searches. New papers enter an existing reading practice: the reader decides whether a paper deserves attention, reconstructs its argument, checks the supporting text, relates it to earlier work, and gradually revises a personal sense of relevance. MNEME was designed around this recurring activity. This chapter explains how the customer evidence was translated into a story map, feature boundaries, a mobile--service architecture, a public interface, and an initial interaction design. It then reports the formative prototype study and the changes that followed from the observed problems.

Three design concerns recur throughout the chapter. First, a paper and the evidence derived from it must remain identifiable even when a later revision appears. Second, generated material and personalized ranking must reveal enough of their basis to support inspection or correction. Third, useful content should remain distinguishable from cached, incomplete, or failed content when network and processing conditions change. These concerns shape the design without prescribing a particular parser, ranking formula, queue, or model. The corresponding implementation methods and their evaluation are presented in Chapter~4.

\section{Storymap and Features}
\label{sec:storymap-features}

The interview and affinity-map findings described in Chapter~2 point to needs both before and after a paper is selected. Participants used titles, abstracts, authors, venues, figures, and code availability to decide what to open, but they also described difficulty recovering useful papers, checking generated explanations, and maintaining awareness of related work. Treating these needs as separate screens would reproduce the fragmentation that the application is intended to reduce. The story map organizes MNEME as one reading loop with five connected stages, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:storymap}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter3_storymap.png}
  \caption{Five-stage story map for the recurring research-reading activity. Paper preparation enables four user-facing experiences: interest-aware briefing, structured understanding, evidence-grounded question answering, and citation-graph exploration.}
  \label{fig:storymap}
\end{figure}

The first stage, receiving new papers, is mainly invisible to the reader but determines what every later screen can claim. MNEME observes a scholarly record, associates it with a stable paper identity and a specific source revision, and prepares the available text. The design does not require every paper to yield a complete representation. When full text cannot be prepared reliably, metadata and an abstract can still support discovery, provided that the reduced evidence level remains visible. This choice favors continuity over silent substitution: a partial paper remains useful, but it is not presented as if all sections had been processed.

The second stage establishes a starting account of the reader's interests. Onboarding asks for a small set of topics and a seed paper, limiting initial effort while giving the first briefing a concrete basis. Later interaction can supply additional evidence, but inferred behavior remains distinct from topics that the reader entered deliberately. The distinction matters because a paper open, a question, and a skipped item can have several explanations. MNEME treats adaptation as revisable assistance rather than a declaration of the user's intent. The interest view provides a direct correction path, and recommendation reasons expose the most relevant basis for an item.

The third stage turns a large candidate stream into a bounded research briefing. A briefing is not designed as an endless feed. It presents a compact ranked set with paper identity, a short account, a relevance indication, and a reason that can be examined during triage. The default weekly rhythm supports periodic orientation, while an explicit refresh accommodates an immediate need. Each briefing is treated as a stable snapshot so that a restored mobile view still represents the same recommendation decision; later preference changes affect a later briefing instead of rewriting an earlier one.

The fourth stage supports close reading through structured paper understanding and paper-scoped question answering. A structured view separates an overview from claims, method, and limitations so that the reader can move quickly between different reading purposes. Question answering narrows retrieval to the selected paper and associates the response with the supplied excerpts. Retrieval-augmented generation combines generation with an external evidence store~\cite{lewis2020rag}; in MNEME, restricting that store to the selected source revision makes the evidence set small enough to inspect. When the available excerpts do not support an answer, the normal result is an explicit insufficient-evidence state rather than a fluent replacement.

The fifth stage places one paper in a bounded citation neighborhood. The graph is intended to answer a practical question---which related paper should the reader inspect next?---rather than to reproduce a complete scholarly network on a phone. A selected node reveals its relationship to the central paper and provides a direct route to the ordinary paper view. Relation and subject filters reduce the visible neighborhood without changing the underlying citation direction, and a selected paper can become the center of the next view. Limiting the neighborhood keeps the display legible and allows graph exploration to rejoin the reading loop instead of becoming a disconnected visualization.

These stages form a cycle rather than a funnel. Preparation makes a paper readable; briefing and search determine which paper receives attention; reading and question asking create new evidence about the reader's interests; graph exploration reveals another candidate; and the next briefing reflects the accumulated, bounded evidence. Stable paper identity connects the cycle. Visible evidence and origin states ensure that a temporary failure or a partial representation changes what the interface says, not which paper the reader believes is being examined.

The core feature set follows directly from this cycle. MNEME requires paper preparation, interest-aware briefings, structured paper views, source-linked questions, and bounded citation exploration because removing any one of them breaks a transition in the intended activity. Search, preference editing, local restoration, and explicit processing states support those transitions. Saving gives a paper a durable place in the reader's local collection, sharing exports its public scholarly identity, and a weekly notification returns to the exact briefing that triggered it. These controls extend the same reading loop across sessions and applications. More speculative modes, such as concept and author graphs, remain useful design explorations but are not necessary to establish the principal loop.

Several criteria deliberately admit a limited outcome. A partial paper, an evidence-based refusal, a bounded graph, and a cached briefing can all be correct responses. Their acceptability depends on whether the limitation is disclosed and whether the next useful action remains clear. This principle avoids a common ambiguity in artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted interfaces, where the presence of generated content may be mistaken for evidence that the underlying operation succeeded completely.

The criteria also separate observable software behavior from empirical quality. Repeated delivery, stable navigation, and disclosed content origin can be assessed against deterministic expectations. Recommendation usefulness, answer quality, and processing performance require different measurements. Chapter~4 turns these feature boundaries into test tasks with expected and actual results, while keeping performance and model behavior as separate evaluation questions.

\section{Engine Architecture}
\label{sec:engine-architecture}

\subsection{System Boundaries and End-to-End Flow}

MNEME separates the latency-sensitive mobile experience from document preparation and model inference. Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture} shows four regions. The Android application owns navigation, presentation, local continuity, and capture of supported interactions. The application service owns the public contract and coordinates domain operations. Durable paper, user, graph, and vector state resides in PostgreSQL with pgvector, while replaceable execution and cache state is handled separately. Scholarly sources and model providers remain external dependencies whose output must cross a validation boundary before it becomes part of the study system.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{chapter3_system_architecture.png}
  \caption{High-level architecture and trust boundaries. The mobile client communicates through a versioned service interface; durable records remain separate from replaceable execution state and validated external inputs.}
  \label{fig:system-architecture}
\end{figure}

This division follows the different lifetimes of the work. Opening a cached briefing or returning to a selected graph node should remain responsive on the phone, whereas acquiring a PDF, extracting text, creating model-derived artifacts, or rebuilding a profile may outlive one screen or request. The client owns interaction continuity, while the service owns operations whose completion must remain meaningful after a process or connection changes. The API communicates that distinction through ordinary resources and explicit processing states.

A paper has two related identities in this design. The logical paper is the stable object used in routes, briefings, events, and graph edges. An observed revision identifies the source content from which a summary, evidence excerpt, or embedding was derived. Keeping these identities separate prevents a later source update from silently changing the basis of an earlier output. User-facing objects can still refer to the familiar paper, while evidence-bearing objects retain the revision needed for interpretation.

The Android side follows a unidirectional flow from a screen action through a ViewModel and repository to a typed presentation state. Network and local records are reconciled before rendering, so a transport response is not itself treated as interface state. This choice supports the offline-first principle that local data should remain immediately readable for features that promise offline access~\cite{androidOfflineFirst2026}. It also lets live, cached, processing, empty, and failed outcomes share the same screen structure without becoming indistinguishable.

Two broader alternatives were considered. A phone-centred architecture would reduce dependence on a service, but document extraction and model inference would compete with mobile memory, battery, and background-execution limits. A single synchronous service call would be simpler to describe, but a connection timeout would then obscure whether work had been accepted, partly completed, or lost. The selected split adds explicit state, but that state supports recovery, evidence lineage, and a clearer mobile experience.

\subsection{Cross-Layer Design Invariants}

The architecture is organized around six design invariants. Stable identity ensures that navigation, events, graph edges, and generated artifacts continue to refer to the intended paper. Revision lineage keeps derived evidence attached to the source content that produced it. Durable acceptance prevents a replaceable worker or cache from becoming the only record of work. Duplicate safety lets a client repeat an uncertain submission without multiplying its effect. Bounded work constrains a document, graph, batch, or generated response before it can exhaust shared resources. Visible degradation ensures that partial or restored content is not presented as a complete live result.

These invariants are intentionally independent of a specific database, queue, or model. For example, replacing the execution system should not change the requirement that accepted long-running work has a stable public identity. Replacing the graph layout should not change the requirement that the selected node maps to a valid paper. Replacing the model should not remove the distinction between a generated claim and the excerpt supplied as support. The invariants provide a common vocabulary for design review across mobile, service, storage, and external-provider boundaries.

Validation occurs at more than one boundary because each boundary protects a different meaning. A downloaded file may be a valid PDF and still provide weak document structure. A graph response may be valid JSON and still contain an edge to an absent node. A generated source marker may refer to a supplied excerpt without establishing that the complete answer is scientifically correct. The design records these outcomes separately. One successful check does not erase a later limitation, and the interface communicates only the strongest status justified by the completed checks.

\subsection{Revision-Aware Document and Evidence Flow}

Figure~\ref{fig:document-dag} presents document preparation at design level. Metadata observation creates or updates a logical paper and identifies the source revision. PDF acquisition and parsing then determine whether full-text evidence is available. Summary generation and evidence-chunk preparation depend on that revision-specific representation, while embedding depends on the prepared chunks. These parallel paths allow independent retry and prevent one failed derivative from invalidating another valid artifact.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{chapter3_document_dag.png}
  \caption{Revision-aware preparation flow. Every derived path remains attached to the observed source revision, and an explicitly partial representation can support limited reading when full-text preparation is unavailable.}
  \label{fig:document-dag}
\end{figure}

The principal design decision is that readiness is a property of the required artifact, not of the request that initiated its preparation. A paper may be ready for metadata display before it is ready for structured reading, and it may be ready for structured reading before evidence embeddings exist for question answering. The service reports the available state without implying that all downstream work succeeded. This model also supports safe reuse: an artifact can be reused only when its paper revision and the method identity needed to interpret it still match the requested operation.

Evidence-grounded question answering uses the same lineage. The reader selects a paper, the service retrieves candidate excerpts only from its selected revision, and the generator receives a bounded evidence set. A conversation identity retains a short history for follow-up questions while preserving the same user and paper scope. The history helps interpret the new question; the current paper excerpts remain the only citable evidence. Returned markers are checked against that set before the interface presents their source records. This design supports local inspection, but it does not treat marker validity as proof that an answer is complete or scientifically true. Retrieved context can coexist with unsupported generated statements~\cite{niu2024ragtruth}, so answer behavior and source support are evaluated separately in Chapter~4.

\subsection{Interest Memory, Recommendation, and Citation Graph}

Interest memory combines explicit and behavioral evidence without collapsing them into one opaque profile. Topics and followed authors are statements that the reader can inspect and edit. Opens, saves, shares, paper-scoped questions, and qualified negative observations are weaker evidence whose interpretation depends on context, recency, repetition, and coverage. The architecture preserves the raw event record and derives a versioned behavioral state from it. This makes adaptation replayable and allows the interface to attenuate or omit behavioral influence when the available evidence is sparse.

Recommendation composes the available interest signals with paper recency and records a concise reason derived from the components that actually participated. Missing evidence does not receive an invented neutral value; the remaining components determine the result within the declared design. An immutable briefing then preserves the ranked papers and reasons shown at that time. This distinction between editable state and historical output is important for correction: changing a topic should alter the next recommendation decision without revising what the application previously displayed.

The citation graph is separate from the interest model even though both can reveal a next paper. Citation observations form directed relationships between papers. Some provider identities may not yet correspond to local paper records, so the design retains unresolved observations without exposing them as fabricated nodes. When a local neighborhood is absent, graph preparation may acquire evidence from scholarly metadata providers or explicit references parsed from the paper; only resolved paper identities become public nodes and edges. A public query then selects a bounded neighborhood around the current paper and may enrich its ordering or grouping. If enrichment is unavailable, a deterministic citation-only view remains possible and is identified as such. Spatial layout and filtering are client concerns; evidence acquisition, identity resolution, and bounded selection remain service concerns.

These two discovery paths serve different intentions. The briefing answers which papers appear relevant to the reader's evolving interests. The graph answers how the selected paper is connected to locally known literature. Keeping the paths distinct makes their explanations more faithful and prevents visual proximity from being interpreted as a personalized score. Their detailed aggregation, ranking, and graph-selection methods belong to Back-end Development in Chapter~4.

\subsection{Mobile State, Recovery, and Event Delivery}
\label{subsec:mobile-state-recovery}

Mobile interaction continues while remote work, local storage, and navigation progress at different rates. A paper screen may receive a complete response, learn that preparation is still running, recover an earlier local record after failure, or have no usable content. These outcomes become separate presentation states with different messages and actions. A late response for a previous route must not replace the newly selected paper, and cancellation caused by navigation must not appear as a service failure.

Graph presentation divides responsibility between a locally packaged WebView and native Android state. The WebView performs the force-directed layout and canvas interaction; the native layer validates the selected identity, presents relationship context, and controls navigation. This division follows the nested-model view that data, task, visual encoding, interaction, and algorithm are separate design questions~\cite{munzner2009nested}. The selected node is retained against the central paper so that opening a neighbor and returning does not erase the reader's position.

Supported behavioral observations use client-generated identities and remain locally queued until the service acknowledges them. A retry can repeat delivery without asking the client to infer whether an earlier request arrived. This mechanism supports continuity, but it does not justify interpreting every absence of interaction as dislike. Event meaning is defined by the behavioral method in Chapter~4; the mobile design is responsible for faithful capture, disclosure, and delivery of the supported actions.

\section{API Design}
\label{sec:api-design}

The public API is the contract between the mobile experience and the service architecture. It uses versioned JSON resources, bearer authentication, stable paper identifiers, and a common error envelope with a request identifier. FastAPI's generated schema describes the study service, while a frozen compatibility specification supports client development against a stable baseline. The design favors resource meaning over internal service names, allowing storage, queue, or provider changes without altering the mobile workflow. Write operations use the same principle of explicit identity. Preference replacement returns the state that was accepted, while event delivery reports which observations were new and which were duplicates. A request that contains an invalid paper or timestamp fails as a coherent batch rather than leaving an ambiguous partial preference update. Read operations use bounded collection sizes and stable ordering so that the client can render an honest empty state and repeat a request without relying on hidden server order.

The contract also limits what a response status means. A healthy API process is not evidence that an external provider is available. A valid answer envelope is not evidence that every generated sentence is supported. A graph with no local neighbors is not evidence that the paper has no citations elsewhere. These distinctions are carried through explicit quality, origin, source-support, processing, and construction fields. The Android client maps those fields into user-facing states instead of reconstructing meaning from a message string.

\section{UI/UX Design}
\label{sec:ui-ux-design}

The initial user interface and user experience (UI/UX) design gives the research briefing a central position because it is the point at which recurring discovery becomes a reading decision. Figure~\ref{fig:ui-flow} shows the information architecture developed for the formative prototype. Onboarding establishes a small explicit profile and one seed paper; the briefing supports triage; paper detail supports structured understanding; and question answering and graph exploration extend from a paper whose identity is already visible. Search and interest editing remain available without interrupting the primary path.

  paper-centred reading, cited question answering, and related-work
  exploration.}

Onboarding uses progressive disclosure. Asking for a small number of topics and one known paper is enough to explain the personalization model without presenting a lengthy settings form before the product has shown value. The interest view remains available after onboarding because an inferred result must be correctable. This follows human--AI interaction guidance that a system should communicate its capabilities and support correction when its interpretation is wrong~\cite{amershi2019guidelines}.

The briefing supports comparison, while paper detail supports close reading.
Each card presents identity, a short description, relevance context, and a
concise reason before the primary open action. The reason appears beside the
ranked item because it is most useful at the decision point. Paper detail then
increases information density and separates overview, claims, method, and
limitations. Source actions remain close to the generated material they
qualify. Position-sensitive reading assistance similarly benefits from
presenting supporting context where the reader encounters the unfamiliar
content~\cite{head2021scholarphi}.

The question screen inherits the selected paper, preserving its scope without
requiring the reader to enter it again. Answer text and cited excerpts form one
inspection unit. After an answer, the same composer accepts a follow-up and
keeps both turns in the paper-scoped conversation. An insufficient-evidence
response occupies the ordinary answer region so that it remains distinct from
a network error.
Figure~\ref{fig:prototype-reading} shows the initial prototype states used
during the moderated sessions. The images use controlled fixture content to
establish the tested interaction design. Chapter~4 presents the implemented
Android application.

Figure~\ref{fig:ui-flow-detail} enlarges the two main parts of the original

\section{Usability Testing}
\label{sec:usability-testing}

The formative study examined whether first-time readers could discover the intended path through the interactive prototype. Five participants completed the same moderated tasks using fixed fixture content. This design held the target controls and information hierarchy constant across sessions. The study supports task-level observations about the tested prototype; it does not measure recommendation quality, answer correctness, runtime performance, or the usability of the later application.

\subsection{Usability Testing Script}
\label{subsec:usability-script}

Each session began by explaining that the interface, rather than the participant, was under evaluation; confusing behavior was useful evidence; and participation could stop at any time. Participants were asked to think aloud. Concurrent think-aloud is widely used in usability testing, and different verbalization patterns vary in how strongly they indicate a usability problem~\cite{fan2019thinkaloud}. The facilitator used the same instruction and non-directive prompts for all sessions and avoided naming the target screen or control.

The five tasks corresponded to transitions in the story map: understanding why a paper was recommended, locating its contribution and one source, asking a paper-scoped question and inspecting the cited evidence, moving from a paper into related work through the graph, and correcting an interest. Table~\ref{tab:usability-script} states the observable criterion used for each task. Pre-session questions covered reading frequency, research area, discovery practice, prior AI-reading tools, and familiarity with citation graphs. Post-session questions asked which component was useful or confusing and whether reasons or citations affected confidence. The retained record supports completion counts and qualitative observations, but it does not contain participant-level timing, taps, hesitation duration, or complete transcripts.

Unaided binary task completion was the primary measure. Qualitative notes were
used to identify the source of a failure and remained descriptive. With five
participants, the study was designed to identify concentrated interaction
problems and guide revision. The analysis therefore reports task counts and
interprets them as observations of discoverability under the moderated
prompts.

\subsection{Usability Tests and Results}
\label{subsec:usability-results}

Figure~\ref{fig:usability-results} reports the recorded completion counts. All five participants completed briefing triage (T1), source inspection (T2), and cited question inspection (T3). Two completed the graph transition (T4), and four saved a preference change (T5). In total, 21 of 25 participant--task attempts met their success criterion, a descriptive proportion of 84\%. The task-level distribution is more informative than the aggregate because it identifies where the initial interaction design required revision.

The 15 completions across T1--T3 indicate that the prompted reading path was discoverable in the prototype. Participants could move from a recommendation to paper detail, locate a source-oriented summary path, and inspect the cited evidence associated with an answer. Session notes also suggested that evidence access deserved a stronger visual cue. The revised design therefore places source labels and citation actions beside the generated content they qualify instead of treating verification as a secondary destination.

Figure~\ref{fig:usability-findings} summarizes the qualitative observations that informed this revision, including the value participants placed on visible evidence and the uncertainty they encountered around verification and graph actions.

\subsection{UI/UX Design Adjustments}
\label{subsec:ui-ux-adjustments}

The design adjustments retain the interactions that participants completed
reliably and strengthen the two transitions that caused uncertainty.
Figure~\ref{fig:ui-design-adjustments} links each observation to a concrete
interface decision. The briefing remains the home screen, and its paper card,
match context, and primary open action are preserved. Source labels and
citation actions move closer to summaries and answers. Preference editing
remains one action from the briefing so that an irrelevant recommendation can
lead directly to correction of the explicit profile.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter3_course_design_adjustments.png}
  \caption{UI/UX adjustments derived from the formative study: retained
  briefing structure, more visible source actions, explicit graph selection,
  and a nearby preference entry point.}
  \label{fig:ui-design-adjustments}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

Graph exploration produced the clearest problem. Three participants did not
infer which elements were selectable or how selection led to another paper.
The adjusted design adds a short instruction above the canvas, a persistent
selected-node state, explicit relationship context, and a visible
\emph{Open Paper} action. These changes are reported as design responses to
the observed ambiguity in action and consequence. Chapter~4 verifies that
selection and navigation follow this specification; a follow-up participant
study would measure the usability effect of the revision. Figure~\ref{fig:graph-design-adjustment} shows how the revised controls make the selection and navigation path explicit.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter3_course_graph_adjustment.png}
  \caption{Graph-specific adjustment from the usability result: an instruction,
  persistent selected-node state, relationship context, and a visible
  \emph{Open Paper} action.}
  \label{fig:graph-design-adjustment}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

One participant did not complete the interest-editing task. The observation indicated that the controls were understandable after discovery but insufficiently prominent from the briefing. The adjusted design therefore keeps preference editing one action away from the briefing and gives it an explicit settings entry. Keeping the entry nearby also gives the reader a practical correction path: when a recommendation reason appears irrelevant, the explicit interests can be inspected and edited without abandoning the reading task.

The sessions support a deliberately limited formative conclusion. Under the moderated tasks, participants could follow the main path through briefing, paper reading, source inspection, and paper-scoped questioning. Graph discoverability required substantial revision, and interest controls required a clearer entry point. These observations explain the design adjustments above, but they do not establish the usability of the revised graph. Broader usability and accessibility claims require a new participant protocol using the implemented interface.
