\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Scientific Work after Search}

Researchers rarely encounter a paper only once.
A paper may first appear in a recommendation feed, later receive a revised preprint, become relevant to a new project, and finally serve as evidence in a technical decision.
During this process, the reader must preserve at least three kinds of continuity.
First, document continuity identifies which paper and revision supplied a statement.
Second, evidential continuity connects a generated explanation to the text that supports it.
Third, interest continuity records why a paper was selected and how later behavior should---or should not---change the system's view of the user's research interests.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) provides a useful foundation because it couples a generator to retrieved non-parametric memory~\cite{lewis2020rag}.
Nevertheless, retrieval does not by itself prove that an answer is supported by the retrieved text.
Citation-oriented evaluation therefore distinguishes answer quality from citation correctness and completeness~\cite{gao2023alce}.
The distinction becomes more important for long scientific documents: language models can use evidence unevenly depending on where it appears in a long context~\cite{liu2024lost}, and chunk-based retrieval can trade global document structure for local factual detail~\cite{zhao2024longrag}.

Personalization introduces a second source of uncertainty.
Opening, saving, skipping, or questioning a paper can be useful feedback, but no single event is a complete statement of research intent.
Recent work on long-term recommendation describes silent engagement and disengagement as unpaired and potentially ambiguous signals~\cite{zhang2025unpaired}.
Other work shows that editable natural-language user profiles can make recommendations more scrutable~\cite{ramos2024scrutable}.
For MNEME, these observations imply that adaptive interest memory must be inspectable and correctable rather than represented as an invisible, authoritative embedding.

The central thesis is consequently narrower than the title's term ``research agent'' may suggest.
MNEME is not claimed to conduct autonomous science.
It is designed as a mobile literature loop whose actions are bounded by versioned paper evidence, declared behavioral signals, and visible user controls.
This framing turns a feature-rich application plan into a falsifiable research program.

\subsection{Observed Workflow Friction}

The project team used ten semi-structured interviews as formative evidence about this downstream workflow.
The participants were students and early-stage researchers from machine learning and natural-language processing, computer vision, and program verification.
The interviews were not designed to estimate population prevalence.
Instead, they asked participants to recount recent search, triage, storage, recall, and verification episodes without first describing MNEME.
The resulting observations repeatedly concerned four forms of discontinuity: excessive candidate volume, difficulty relocating previously encountered papers, fragmented personal organization, and uncertainty about unsupported AI summaries.
Within this ten-person formative sample, eight participants described difficulty retrieving previously encountered papers, seven described excessive paper volume, seven described weak connections between papers and developing ideas, and five raised concerns about trusting AI-generated material without inspectable sources.
These counts characterize the interviewed group only; they are used to prioritize design attention rather than to estimate the prevalence of each problem in the wider research community.
These observations are reported as small-sample design evidence in Chapter~2 rather than as universal claims about researchers.

The motivation for a mobile client follows from when these discontinuities occur.
Triage, saving, sharing, and recalling papers are often interleaved with meetings, commuting, messages from collaborators, and short gaps between other tasks.
A mobile interface can shorten the path from an alert to an inspectable paper record, but mobility also creates stricter constraints on attention, connectivity, screen area, and background execution.
The value of a mobile research assistant therefore cannot be established merely by reproducing a desktop chatbot on a smaller display.
The system must decide what information remains local, what work may be deferred, which evidence must stay one action away, and how stale or seeded content is distinguished from live content.

\section{Problem Statement}

The central problem addressed in this thesis is \emph{continuous literature understanding}: preserving a usable and inspectable relationship among evolving papers, generated assistance, and evolving research interests across repeated sessions.
The word ``continuous'' has three meanings.
It refers to continuity across document revisions, because a summary or cited answer must identify the exact version from which it was derived.
It refers to continuity across evidence transformations, because a retrieved passage, a generated claim, and the source action shown on the phone must describe the same evidential object.
It also refers to continuity across user interactions, because a later recommendation should be explainable in terms of declared interests and bounded behavioral events rather than an opaque profile update.

This problem is not equivalent to paper discovery.
A discovery engine may return a highly relevant paper and still leave the researcher to judge it, remember it, reconnect it to earlier work, and verify any machine-produced explanation.
Nor is it equivalent to summarization.
A fluent summary can reduce reading time while increasing epistemic risk if its claims cannot be traced to the current paper revision.
Finally, the problem is not equivalent to generic personalization.
Optimizing engagement without preserving signal semantics may cause temporary curiosity, course work, or accidental taps to become persistent research interests.

MNEME addresses the combined problem through a bounded loop:
\emph{receive} new papers, \emph{read} a revision-aware representation, \emph{engage} through evidence-linked assistance, \emph{remember} explicit and behavioral signals, \emph{plan} a limited briefing, and \emph{connect} the current paper to a bounded citation neighborhood.
The loop is successful only when the transitions remain inspectable.
An answer that loses its evidence, a recommendation that hides its reason, or a graph that provides no clear next action breaks the loop even if the underlying component appears technically functional.

Four connected technical challenges make this problem difficult.
The first is revision-correct paper representation.
A paper identifier is insufficient when its source document can change while summaries, chunks, embeddings, and citations remain attached to an older revision.
The system must therefore preserve a lineage from a logical paper to an observed revision and from that revision to every derived artifact, invalidating or regenerating an artifact when its evidential basis changes.

The second challenge is evidence-bounded assistance.
A generated answer may contain a valid paper identifier while one or more of its claims remain unsupported.
Scientific claim-verification research makes the evidence unit explicit by pairing claims with rationale spans~\cite{wadden2020scifact}, and fine-grained attribution work shows why document-level identifiers alone make exact support difficult to inspect~\cite{huang2024grounded}.
MNEME consequently separates identifier validity, evidence availability, claim--evidence consistency, and evidence coverage, and treats refusal as a valid outcome when the available text does not support an answer.

The third challenge is adaptive but inspectable interest memory.
Opening, saving, skipping, and questioning a paper are incomplete and sometimes ambiguous expressions of intent.
MNEME must combine explicit interests with bounded behavioral events without silently turning temporary curiosity or accidental interaction into a durable research preference.
The user therefore needs to see why an interest changed and to correct or delete the corresponding state.

The fourth challenge is bounded mobile integration.
Intermittent connectivity, restricted background execution, notification burden, and limited screen area can conceal evidence or stale state even when the server-side mechanism is correct.
The mobile workflow must preserve paper identity, evidence access, and event semantics while visibly distinguishing live, cached, fixture, fallback, and error states.
These challenges are dependent rather than isolated: revision errors can contaminate retrieval, duplicated mobile events can distort later ranking, and an interface that hides degraded operation can convert a recoverable limitation into a misleading research interaction.

The proposed workflow is organized around three reader-facing actions: read, remember, and plan.
Reading begins with a paced digest rather than an unbounded result list and retains links from concise explanations to the relevant paper sections or figures.
Remembering keeps saved and previously read papers retrievable while associating them with explicit topics and inspectable behavioral signals.
Planning uses this durable state to assemble a limited briefing and to expose related work through a bounded graph instead of requiring the researcher to reconstruct relationships manually.
These actions translate the interview findings into system behavior without treating the formative counts as effectiveness results.

The storyboard in Figure~\ref{fig:storyboard-source} makes this intended sequence concrete.
An early-stage researcher first encounters an unmanageable set of candidate papers.
A mobile notification then presents a deliberately limited weekly digest.
The researcher opens one item, reads a concise explanation, and follows section- and figure-level links to inspect the evidence.
After saving the paper, she can see its place in a personal research graph and later retrieve the paper and its supporting evidence during a group discussion.
This sequence is a design scenario that connects the identified problems to proposed interactions; it is not evidence that the complete longitudinal workflow has already been achieved.

\section{Existing Solutions and Their Drawbacks}

\subsection{Search Engines and Reference Managers}

Traditional literature workflows combine search engines, publisher sites, local folders, spreadsheets, and reference managers.
These tools remain important because they provide broad coverage, familiar bibliographic records, and user-controlled collections.
The formative interviews likewise show that participants already rely on Google Scholar, arXiv, Zotero, Mendeley, browser bookmarks, local folders, and personal notes.
The limitation is not that these tools fail at their intended functions.
It is that the user must manually preserve the path from discovery to later comprehension.
Folders and tags can degrade into inconsistent archives; bibliographic records do not necessarily preserve claim-level reading context; and a paper found through one channel may be summarized or discussed in another without shared provenance.

\subsection{General-Purpose Language Models and Paper Assistants}

General-purpose language models and paper-oriented assistants can summarize documents and answer questions with low interaction cost.
RAG improves this workflow by supplying retrieved non-parametric context to generation~\cite{lewis2020rag}.
However, retrieval success, answer quality, citation validity, and claim support are distinct properties.
ALCE makes citation correctness and completeness explicit rather than treating the presence of a citation marker as sufficient~\cite{gao2023alce}.
SciFact similarly represents scientific verification using claims and evidence rationales~\cite{wadden2020scifact}.
For MNEME, these findings rule out a weak definition of ``verified'' in which an answer merely contains a valid paper identifier.
The interface must expose a passage-level path, and the evaluation must record unsupported, partially supported, and no-evidence cases.

\subsection{Recommendation Feeds and Knowledge Graphs}

Recommendation systems can reduce candidate volume, while citation graphs can reveal neighboring work.
Yet both can become opaque.
A recommendation score may conceal which explicit topic or behavior affected ranking, and a large graph may be visually impressive without helping a user choose a next paper.
Research on scrutable profiles suggests that editable representations can support user inspection and correction~\cite{ramos2024scrutable}, while research on unpaired feedback warns that silence or disengagement does not have a single stable meaning~\cite{zhang2025unpaired}.
The formative graph test in this project supplies a complementary interaction warning: only two of five prototype participants completed the related-paper graph task, although the graph concept itself attracted interest.
The combined lesson is that adaptation and visualization must terminate in understandable actions and reversible state.

\subsection{Unmet Combined Design Space}

Existing categories therefore solve valuable portions of the workflow, but their boundaries leave a combined gap.
Search engines optimize discovery; reference managers organize records; language-model tools assist comprehension; recommender systems prioritize candidates; and graph tools expose relationships.
Continuous literature understanding requires these portions to share identity, evidence, and user-state contracts.
MNEME's research gap is not the absence of any one feature.
It is the absence, within the supplied project scope, of an evaluated mobile loop in which exact-revision provenance, evidence-bounded claims, correctable interest memory, and bounded graph interaction are jointly visible.

\section{Proposed Solution}

\subsection{Research Questions}

The challenges lead to four research questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.3cm,style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1] How can a mobile-oriented literature system preserve revision-correct paper identity and section-level provenance during ingestion?
  \item[RQ2] How can retrieval and generation be constrained so that scientific answers remain inspectable against retrieved paper evidence?
  \item[RQ3] How can explicit interests and bounded implicit behavior update a user model without hiding or overstating what the system has learned?
  \item[RQ4] Can these mechanisms be integrated into a usable mobile research loop within stated latency, reliability, and privacy boundaries?
\end{description}

RQ2 and RQ3 are the primary questions.
RQ1 supplies the evidence substrate on which RQ2 depends, while RQ4 tests whether the three mechanisms survive end-to-end integration.

\subsection{Contributions and Evaluation Approach}

The first contribution is a provenance contract: each derived artifact is associated with a logical paper, a specific revision, a transformation configuration, and a content hash.
The second contribution is an evidence-bounded assistance contract: answers are decomposed into claims, claims point to source spans, and verification distinguishes different failure types.
The third contribution is a bounded adaptation contract: declared behavioral events update a time-decayed normalized representation while users can inspect or correct the resulting state.
The fourth contribution is an integration and evaluation framework that connects these mechanisms to a mobile research workflow.
Together, the evaluations examine revision isolation, evidence retrieval and citation support, controlled adaptation, and mobile reliability and usability.
Each result is interpreted only at the scope established by its frozen inputs, software revision, configuration, and retained raw artifacts.

\subsection{Scope and Claim Boundary}

The available materials include a controlling thesis outline, product-level acceptance criteria, architecture and API designs, a story map, an interactive HTML prototype, UI/UX mockups, a customer-research report, a mid-term implementation report, a formative-usability presentation, and an inspected source repository with exact implementation snapshots.
These sources support a fuller distinction than the earlier draft.
The customer-research method and prototype outcomes are \observed{} but \limited{} because the local package does not include full transcripts or participant-level task tables.
The mid-term report and inspected repository identify executable backend, API, and Android artifacts at stated cutoffs.
The available Android measurements establish bounded behavior on the stated emulator and configuration, while the formative usability counts remain limited because participant-level timing and interaction records are unavailable.
Accordingly, Chapters 2 and 3 distinguish historical design evidence from inspected implementation, Chapter 4 reports only retained measurements, and Chapter 5 does not generalize beyond those results.

\subsection{Thesis Structure}

Chapter 2 translates the four challenges into functional, non-functional, ethical, and interaction requirements.
Chapter 3 defines the application design and the cross-cutting contracts that connect its features.
Chapter 4 explains the implemented front-end and back-end mechanisms before reporting the retained testing results.
Chapter 5 answers each research question at the strength permitted by the completed evidence.
