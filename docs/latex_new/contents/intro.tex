\section{Background}

Researchers rarely encounter a paper only once.
A paper may first appear in a recommendation feed, later receive a revised preprint, become relevant to a new project, and finally serve as evidence in a technical decision.
Work on a paper can therefore span discovery, rapid triage, close reading, and later reuse.
The information needed at each stage differs: an abstract may support an initial decision, while a later argument may depend on methods or evidence in the full text.
A revised preprint can also alter the text on which an earlier summary or note was based, so later reuse depends on knowing which version was read.
The scholarly record is also an evolving network of publications, authors, institutions, and citations, so the value of a paper often emerges from its relation to other work and to a changing research question~\cite{fortunato2018science}.
This activity is a form of sensemaking: the reader searches for a useful representation, encodes selected information into it, and revises that representation to answer task-specific questions~\cite{russell1993sensemaking}.
Personal information management research also shows that people combine bookmarks, files, printed pages, and other keeping methods to support later reuse~\cite{jones2002keeping}.
The resulting literature record is distributed across tools and sessions, and its usefulness depends on whether the reader can later reconstruct the context in which a paper was selected and understood.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) offers one approach to AI-assisted literature work by coupling a generator to retrieved non-parametric memory~\cite{lewis2020rag}.
RAG makes external text available during generation and creates a path by which an explanation may be related to its source.
Scientific papers remain demanding inputs because they are long, structured documents in which an argument may depend on material distributed across sections, figures, and equations.
Language models can use evidence unevenly across a long context~\cite{liu2024lost}, while chunk-based retrieval can trade global document structure for local factual detail~\cite{zhao2024longrag}.
Reading-interface research identifies a related locality problem from a human perspective: definitions needed for comprehension may appear in another section or paper, and position-sensitive access can reduce the effort required to recover them~\cite{head2021scholarphi}.
Scientific reading consequently involves repeated movement between a concise explanation and the detailed evidence needed to assess it.
The source paper, its internal structure, and the position of the supporting material all contribute to the meaning of an AI-assisted explanation.
These characteristics make document structure and source location essential parts of the literature-reading context.

Personalization offers another way to reduce the effort of following a large literature.
Opening, saving, skipping, or questioning a paper can provide useful feedback, although no single event fully expresses research intent.
Research on long-term recommendation has used continued engagement and silent disengagement as distinct feedback signals~\cite{zhang2025unpaired}.
Editable natural-language user profiles provide another way for readers to inspect and revise the assumptions behind a recommendation~\cite{ramos2024scrutable}.
The meaning of an interaction also depends on its setting: a paper may be opened for a course, a temporary collaboration, or a research direction that later changes.
Explicit profile statements capture deliberate choices at a particular time, whereas behavior supplies continuing but less direct observations.
Longitudinal personalization therefore draws on signals produced at different times and under changing research goals.
Over time, the observable interaction history and the current estimate of interest remain distinct sources of information.

Literature work can also continue beyond a desktop session.
On a mobile device, a researcher may triage, save, share, or recall papers during a brief interval between other tasks.
Such sessions are easily interrupted and may later resume under different network conditions.
Mobile platforms must also account for offline data access and restrictions on persistent background work~\cite{androidOfflineFirst2026,androidWorkManager2026}.
Information prepared by a remote service may arrive after the user has changed screens, while locally retained information may remain available when the network is absent.
The client must consequently coordinate information that is produced at different times and may have different levels of freshness.
The mobile setting therefore joins the cognitive problem of recovering research context with the platform problem of preserving state across delayed and interrupted operations.
Taken together, these conditions make literature work longitudinal: the paper may change, explanations must remain connected to evidence, research interests evolve, and reading sessions may be interrupted and resumed.

\section{Problem Statement}

The central problem addressed in this thesis is \emph{continuous literature understanding}: preserving usable and inspectable links among changing paper versions, AI-generated summaries and answers, evolving research interests, and mobile reading state across repeated sessions.
The problem comprises four related forms of continuity.
Representation continuity keeps each derived artifact tied to the paper revision from which it was produced.
Evidence continuity connects a generated claim to the source passage available for inspection.
Interest continuity relates later recommendations to explicit preferences and recorded reading behavior.
Interaction continuity preserves the selected paper and the status of its data when a mobile session is interrupted or resumed.

These continuity requirements become important after a paper is discovered.
Once a paper enters a researcher's workflow, it may be assessed, summarized, connected to related work, and revisited in later sessions.
A useful summary must remain tied to its source revision and supporting evidence, while an interest model must distinguish persistent research intent from temporary or accidental interaction.
The same paper may consequently participate in several later decisions, each of which depends on information recorded at an earlier stage.
When paper identity, evidence, interest context, or visible interaction state is lost during a transition, a later output may appear plausible while referring to the wrong revision, unsupported text, an inaccurate account of the reader's intent, or unavailable data whose status is unclear.

Meeting these requirements raises four connected research challenges.
The first technical challenge is revision-correct paper representation.
A source document can change while summaries, chunks, embeddings, and citations remain attached to an older revision.
A paper identifier alone does not reveal this difference.
The difficulty extends beyond detecting that a new file exists, because each derived artifact has its own creation time, configuration, and dependence on the source text.
An unchanged logical identifier can therefore refer to several evidential states over the lifetime of a paper.
The challenge is to preserve a lineage from a logical paper to an observed revision and from that revision to every derived artifact, including the conditions under which an artifact becomes outdated.
Without this lineage, later retrieval and generation cannot determine whether two apparently compatible artifacts were produced from the same evidence.

The second technical challenge is evidence-bounded assistance.
It must separate the presence of a citation from the support that the cited evidence provides.
A generated answer may contain a valid paper identifier while one or more of its claims remain unsupported.
Scientific claim-verification research makes the evidence unit explicit by pairing claims with rationale spans~\cite{wadden2020scifact}, and fine-grained attribution work shows why document-level identifiers alone make exact support difficult to inspect~\cite{huang2024grounded}.
Retrieval also introduces several possible failure points: no relevant passage may be found, a relevant passage may be omitted from the final context, or a generated claim may extend beyond the retrieved text.
The challenge is to distinguish identifier validity, evidence availability, claim--evidence consistency, and evidence coverage, including cases in which the available text does not support an answer.
This distinction is necessary because a syntactically valid citation marker provides weaker assurance than a source span that supports the associated claim.

The third technical challenge is adaptive but inspectable interest memory.
It must interpret incomplete and sometimes ambiguous expressions of intent.
Opening, saving, skipping, and questioning a paper differ in strength and duration: saving commonly indicates deliberate interest, while a single opening may reflect curiosity, an assignment, or an accidental action.
Repeated events can provide additional evidence, although frequency alone does not resolve the context in which they occurred.
The challenge is to combine explicit interests with bounded behavioral events while preserving a clear account of why an interest changed and allowing the corresponding state to be corrected or deleted.
The resulting representation must therefore support adaptation without treating an inferred preference as an unquestionable statement of the user's research agenda.

The fourth technical challenge is bounded mobile integration.
It must preserve research context under intermittent connectivity, restricted background execution, and limited screen area.
These conditions can conceal evidence or stale state even when the server-side mechanism is correct.
Long-running preparation and graph operations may complete after the user has navigated elsewhere, and a retry may repeat an interaction that has already reached the server.
Cached content can preserve useful access during failure, yet it can also mislead the reader when its origin and age are unclear.
The challenge is to preserve paper identity, evidence access, and interaction meaning while communicating whether the displayed result is current, delayed, degraded, or unavailable.
The challenges interact: revision errors can contaminate retrieval, duplicated mobile events can distort later ranking, and an interface that hides degraded operation can convert a recoverable limitation into a misleading research interaction.
Solving any challenge in isolation therefore leaves the overall literature workflow vulnerable at the boundaries between components.

\subsection{Cross-Layer Continuity Model}

The four challenges can be stated as continuity conditions over a single reading transition.
Let the state at time \(t\) be
\begin{equation}
  X_t=(p,r,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},U,V),
  \label{eq:continuity-state}
\end{equation}
where \(p\) is the logical paper, \(r\) its observed revision, \(\mathcal{A}\) the set of derived artifacts, \(\mathcal{E}\) the displayed claim--source links, \(U\) the explicit and behavioral preference state, and \(V\) the visible client state.
A transition \(T_t:X_t\rightarrow X_{t+1}\) preserves continuity when four predicates hold:
\begin{equation}
  C(T_t)=C_R(T_t)\land C_E(T_t)\land C_U(T_t)\land C_I(T_t).
  \label{eq:continuity-invariant}
\end{equation}

The representation predicate \(C_R\) requires the artifacts and source links used in a transition to retain a compatible \((p,r)\) lineage.
The evidence predicate \(C_E\) requires each displayed source marker to resolve to evidence used for the associated claim.
The preference predicate \(C_U\) requires a change in user state, together with a later recommendation reason, to be traceable to declared topics, recorded events, and the stated reference time.
The interaction predicate \(C_I\) requires the selected paper identity and the availability of its data to remain visible across a client transition.
A failure of any predicate identifies a specific break in continuity even when the other parts of the workflow remain operational.
The conjunction is deliberately strict: success in one layer does not compensate for a lost paper revision, an unsupported claim, an untraceable preference change, or an ambiguous client state in another layer.

\subsection{Research Questions}

The continuity conditions translate the four challenges into the following
research questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.3cm,style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1] How can a literature system preserve revision-correct paper identity and section-level provenance throughout ingestion and the creation of derived artifacts?
  \item[RQ2] How can retrieval and generation be constrained so that readers can check paper-specific answers against retrieved source passages?
  \item[RQ3] How can explicit interests and bounded behavioral signals update a user model while keeping each change inspectable and correctable?
  \item[RQ4] Can these mechanisms be integrated into a mobile research loop that preserves paper identity, evidence access, and interaction meaning while clearly exposing live, cached, processing, and failure states?
\end{description}

The questions are coupled through the flow of paper identity, evidence, and user state across the system.
RQ1 and RQ2 are linked because a claim can be checked against its source only after the paper revision and retrieved passage are fixed.
RQ3 addresses how later interactions change recommendation state.
RQ4 asks whether paper identity, evidence access, interaction meaning, and operational state remain clear when work crosses the backend and mobile client.

\section{Existing Solutions and Their Drawbacks}

Traditional literature workflows combine search engines, publisher sites, local folders, spreadsheets, and reference managers.
These tools remain important because they provide broad coverage, familiar bibliographic records, and user-controlled collections.
Search services support query-based discovery, citation navigation, and alerts, while reference managers support bibliographic organization, annotation, synchronization, and citation export~\cite{googleScholarHelp2026,zoteroDocs2026,zoteroSync2026}.
Together, they provide a reliable foundation for locating papers and maintaining a collection.
The limitation appears at the transition from discovery to later comprehension, where the user must preserve context manually.
Folders and tags can degrade into inconsistent archives; bibliographic records do not necessarily preserve claim-level reading context; and a paper found through one channel may be summarized or discussed in another without shared provenance.
The later reader may recover the file and its citation metadata while still lacking the reason it mattered, the passage that supported an earlier conclusion, or the relationship between that conclusion and a newer revision.
This category therefore addresses discovery and organization more directly than the continuity of interpretation across repeated sessions.

General-purpose language models and paper-oriented assistants can summarize documents and answer questions about supplied text.
RAG supports this workflow by supplying retrieved non-parametric context during generation~\cite{lewis2020rag}.
For a reader, this interaction can shorten the path from a broad question to a candidate explanation and a potentially relevant passage.
However, retrieval success, answer quality, citation validity, and claim support are distinct properties.
ALCE evaluates citation correctness and completeness in addition to the presence of citation markers~\cite{gao2023alce}.
SciFact similarly represents scientific verification using claims and evidence rationales~\cite{wadden2020scifact}.
Consequently, an assistant that displays a valid paper identifier can still leave the support for an individual claim unclear.
Document updates introduce another dependency because an answer produced from one revision may remain readable after its source has changed.
Paper assistants improve immediate access to document content, while longitudinal use also requires an explicit relationship among the source revision, retrieved passage, generated claim, and later display.

Recommendation systems can reduce candidate volume, while citation graphs can reveal neighboring work.
Implicit-feedback methods distinguish an observed action from the confidence with which it represents preference~\cite{hu2008implicit}, and temporal recommendation models account for changes in user preferences and item effects~\cite{koren2009temporal}.
A recommendation score may nevertheless conceal which explicit topic or behavior affected ranking, and a large graph does not by itself help a reader choose a next paper.
Research on scrutable profiles suggests that editable representations can support user inspection and correction~\cite{ramos2024scrutable}. Work on unpaired feedback shows how continued engagement and silent disengagement can provide positive and negative training signals without explicit paired feedback~\cite{zhang2025unpaired}.
Citation-graph tools complement ranking by exposing relationships that are difficult to express as a query, including shared references and neighboring works~\cite{connectedPapersAbout2026}.
Their usefulness still depends on selection and navigation: the reader needs to identify the current paper, understand what an edge represents, and continue from a selected node.
These approaches still require an interaction design that makes adaptation understandable and turns graph relationships into clear paper-level actions.
The remaining limitation concerns how ranking evidence, graph context, and later user actions are carried into the next reading session.

Existing categories therefore solve valuable portions of the workflow, but their boundaries leave a combined gap.
Search engines optimize discovery; reference managers organize records; language-model tools assist comprehension; recommender systems prioritize candidates; and graph tools expose relationships.
Continuous literature understanding requires these portions to share identity, evidence, and user-state contracts.
The remaining research gap lies in the handoffs among these functions.
An answer needs the same paper and revision identity established during preparation; a recommendation reason needs the interest state produced by earlier interactions; and a graph selection needs a stable route back to the selected paper.
Without shared contracts, each component can succeed locally while the reader loses the context needed to inspect or resume the broader task.
A system addressing the combined problem must therefore preserve this context whenever work passes from one function to another.

\section{Proposed Solution}

\subsection{System Overview}

MNEME is a mobile-native research agent that supports a bounded literature workflow from paper preparation to later retrieval.
Here, a research agent coordinates paper preparation, evidence-linked reading, preference-aware briefing, and related-paper exploration while the researcher retains responsibility for interpretation and use.
Its core technical idea is to preserve the information required to inspect and resume work when a reader moves between activities.
The paper identifier and revision, source evidence, preference history, and client availability state therefore remain explicit throughout the workflow.
These shared contracts give each component a defined responsibility while preserving the relationships on which later operations depend.

When a paper enters the system, preparation records its logical identity and observed revision before producing sections, chunks, and other derived artifacts.
Briefings present a limited set of candidates together with recommendation reasons.
Opening a paper retains the same identity for summaries and question answering, where answers expose supporting source passages or report that the available evidence is insufficient.
Explicit interests and recorded interactions update a traceable preference state.
A citation neighborhood allows a selected node to lead back to the corresponding paper, while the mobile client distinguishes live, cached, processing, and failure states.
The resulting sequence carries the context of a paper from its initial encounter into later reading and reuse.

Figure~\ref{fig:core-capabilities} summarizes the proposed organization.
The four capabilities remain useful as separate user actions, while their shared continuity contracts preserve the relationships needed when work moves from one capability to another.
Revision-aware preparation supplies the identity and artifacts used by evidence-linked reading.
Inspectable interest memory supplies the traceable preference state used by briefings, and bounded exploration returns the reader to a paper whose identity remains available to the other capabilities.
The central block identifies the information shared across these capabilities: paper identity, evidence status, behavioral history, and visible client state.

\subsection{Contributions and Evaluation Approach}

The provenance contract associates each derived artifact with a logical paper, a specific revision, a transformation configuration, and a content hash.
Evidence-bounded assistance links answer claims to source spans and distinguishes supported, partially supported, and unsupported outcomes.
Interest adaptation applies declared behavioral events to a time-decayed normalized representation while retaining the information needed for inspection and correction.
Mobile integration preserves selected paper identity, event meaning, and content availability across asynchronous operations and recovery.
Together, these contributions supply stable evidence inputs, constrain generated assistance, maintain a traceable interest state, and keep the resulting state visible during interaction.

The evaluation is organized around the four technical challenges.
Revision-isolation tests examine provenance; paper-specific question-answering experiments measure retrieval, citation support, and refusal behavior; controlled traces examine interest adaptation; and Android measurements assess mobile reliability and graph interaction.
The ten-interview customer study and five-participant prototype study complement these technical evaluations by informing the requirements and interaction design.
Software tests verify specified behavior, while controlled and live measurements characterize the implemented mechanisms under documented configurations.
