\chapter{Design Specification}

This chapter translates the customer-research evidence into requirements for
MNEME.  The formative study combines interviews, affinity mapping, customer
profile synthesis, and competitor analysis with a small computing-focused
sample.  The resulting evidence supports the design decisions within that
scope.  Chapter~3 develops the system and interface design, and Chapter~4
reports implementation and testing.

\section{Customer Requirements}

The customer study examined how researchers decide which papers deserve
attention and how they later recover and connect those papers.  It also covered
the verification of machine-generated explanations and the maintenance of a
useful reading routine.  The resulting requirements concern the whole reading
process.  Paper identity and evidence must remain visible,
recommendations must be bounded, and the mobile application must recover from
interruption.  The reader also needs clear actions and control over
personalization.

\subsection{User Interview Questionnaire}

The questionnaire was designed to elicit recent behavior before participants
were shown the proposed application.  This order reduced feature-led responses
and kept the discussion focused on existing research practice.  The eleven
open-ended questions covered research context, reading frequency, current
tools, a recent discovery episode, information overload, paper selection,
storage, recall failure, relationships among papers, and trust in
AI-generated summaries and answers.  The complete instrument appears in
Appendix~\ref{app:interview}.

The sequence moved from general context to specific episodes.  Questions such
as ``Tell me about the last time you found an important paper'' asked
participants to describe concrete decisions and breakdowns.  Interviewers used
neutral follow-up prompts, including ``What happened next?'', ``What made that
difficult?'', and ``How did it affect your work?''.  These prompts helped
separate observed workflow problems from reactions to a proposed feature.

The instrument served three design purposes.  Questions about discovery and
selection identified the information needed for rapid triage.  Questions about
storage and recall examined how paper identity and personal organization
survive over time.  Questions about connections and trust examined the need
for related-paper navigation, visible evidence, and control over automated
assistance.  The questionnaire provided a consistent structure across
interviews while leaving space for practices that had not been anticipated
during instrument design.

The eleven questions also support a trace from evidence to design.  Q1 and Q2
establish field and reading frequency, which qualify later answers about paper
volume.  Q3 maps the existing tool set.  Q4--Q7 examine discovery episodes and
triage under overload.  Q8 and Q9 examine storage and later recovery.  Q10
examines how relationships among papers are maintained.  Q11 asks what would
make a generated summary or answer sufficiently trustworthy to use in research.
The study therefore covers the full path from first encounter to later
verification and recall.

The instrument did not include a product pitch, ranking of proposed features,
or forced choice among interface concepts.  This boundary is important for the
interpretation of the results.  A participant's mention of lost papers, manual
source checking, or fragmented notes arose before a corresponding MNEME feature
was described.  The interviews support the existence of these workflow
problems within the sample.  They do not establish demand for the final
interface or measure the effectiveness of the implemented system.

\subsection{Customer Interviews}

The customer study comprised ten semi-structured interviews, identified as
P1--P10.  Participants ranged from first-year master's students to doctoral
researchers and a lecturer.  Their reported areas included machine learning
and natural language processing, computer vision, and program verification.
Recruitment took place through laboratories, paper-reading groups, and campus
study areas.  Sessions were conducted individually in person or by video call
and lasted approximately fifteen minutes on average.  Interview notes were
converted into single-observation statements after each session for later
affinity mapping.

Participants described broad discovery practices.  Google Scholar, arXiv,
advisor or colleague referrals, mailing lists, and general-purpose AI tools
were reported across the interviews.  Paper-management practices included
reference managers, local folders, browser bookmarks, spreadsheets, and note
systems.  Selection decisions drew on title, abstract, author, venue, citation
context, recency, and recommendations from trusted people.  The range of tools
indicates that MNEME must preserve original links and bibliographic identity
and must fit around established research practices.

Coding these practices provides additional detail about their relative weight.
Google Scholar appeared in seven participant records and arXiv in six.  Four
participants described receiving papers through advisors or other human
contacts.  Reference managers included Mendeley in three records and Zotero in
two; other participants used ordinary folders, spreadsheets, browser bookmarks,
or a personal note page.  These observations describe a mixed workflow in which
discovery, document storage, and personal interpretation are handled by
different tools.

Triage began with the paper itself.  Seven participant records mention reading
the abstract, and five mention figures or tables.  Venue or journal reputation
and familiarity with an author or research group each appear in four records;
availability of code appears in three.  This ordering influenced the mobile
information hierarchy.  The paper card gives priority to title, authorship,
category, a concise account, and a recommendation reason, while the paper page
provides the longer method, claim, and source information needed after the
initial decision.

Differences in publication culture did not remove the common downstream
problem.  The machine-learning and computer-vision participants described
fast-moving preprint streams.  Program-verification work was associated with a
slower and more proof-oriented publication cycle.  Participants across these
areas still reported later recovery, personal organization, and evidence
checking difficulties.  This convergence motivated a design centered on the
state that persists after discovery, with category-specific ranking left as a
separate configurable concern.

Paper volume was a recurring problem, which led to the requirement for a
limited briefing with an intelligible reason for each selection.  Recovery
failures created a second requirement: stable paper identity and reading
context must persist across the application even when the reader remembers an
idea but not its title, author, or storage location.  Participants who used AI
assistance also described manual source checking as necessary.  Summaries and
answers consequently need evidence actions close to the claims they qualify.

The study provides formative evidence for this design.  The sample is small,
non-random, and concentrated in computing-related disciplines.  The analysis
used interview notes converted into observation statements; no transcript-level
coding protocol was applied.  Counts in the following subsection describe
coded observation units and cannot support population-level prevalence
estimates.

\subsection{Affinity Map}

Each reported observation was assigned to a digital note and tagged with its
participant code.  The notes were clustered into subthemes and then into four
top-level groups: Pain Points, Current Solutions, Trust in AI, and Wishes.  The
affinity map contains 36 pain-point notes, 74 current-solution notes, 21
trust-related notes, and 2 wish notes.  A participant could contribute several
notes to one group, so these totals describe the coding structure and do not
represent numbers of participants.  Figure~\ref{fig:affinity-map} presents the
four themes across six enlarged panels.  Pain Points and Current Solutions are
divided into two panels each so that the individual observations remain
legible.

The pain-point clusters describe information overload, difficulty recovering a
previously encountered paper, deterioration of personal storage systems, and
weak support for maintaining relationships among papers.  Participants
described episodes in which they remembered an idea without a searchable title
or author, spent substantial time recovering one paper, or asked a colleague
to identify it.  They also described folders or topic lists that became
difficult to search because filenames and metadata were inconsistent.  These
observations connect discovery with later comprehension and recall.

The 36 pain-point notes divide into four subthemes in the coding analysis.
Information overload contains 12 notes, later paper recovery contains 10,
missing links among papers and ideas contains 9, and storage deterioration
contains 5.  Four participant records describe conference or deadline periods
that produce tens or hundreds of candidate papers in a week.  Two records
describe searches lasting from twenty minutes to one hour for a paper that had
already been encountered.  Three participants report that they do not maintain
a citation or concept map of their own.  The counts refer to coded observation
units, and a participant may contribute to several subthemes.

The distinction between recovery and storage is useful for design.  Storage
answers the question of whether a file or record was retained.  Recovery asks
whether the user can reconstruct enough identity and context to locate it
later.  A folder can satisfy the first condition while failing the second.
MNEME therefore uses stable paper identity across the briefing, detail,
question, cache, and graph views.  The design also retains recommendation
reasons and source context, which provide additional retrieval cues when a
title or author has been forgotten.

The Current Solutions cluster shows that participants already combine several
specialized tools.  Search services support discovery, reference managers
retain bibliographic records, folders store documents, and note systems retain
personal interpretation.  Transitions between these tools require manual
work.  MNEME therefore concentrates on the transitions from recommendation to
reading, from generated explanation to source evidence, and from a paper to a
small related-paper neighborhood.

The Current Solutions group is the largest at 74 notes.  Its size does not
indicate satisfaction with one existing product.  The group is large because
participants combined separate practices to complete the workflow.  The coding
scheme assigns 28 notes to discovery channels, 13 to management and note tools,
and 33 to decisions about whether a paper deserves attention.  Existing search
and reference tools remain part of the workflow.  MNEME preserves canonical
paper links and focuses on a mobile handoff from a bounded recommendation to
evidence-linked reading and later retrieval.

The Trust in AI cluster emphasizes inspectability.  Participants wanted to
check important statements against the source paper and expressed concern
about confident answers without visible support.  This finding leads to
source-linked summaries, evidence-bearing question answering, and distinct
states for missing, partial, and available evidence.

Within the 21 trust-related notes, 13 belong to the traceability subtheme.
Five participant records ask for the exact section, figure, or page; three
describe distrust of summaries without a source pointer; and two state that
important claims should be checked against the original.  Eight notes describe
other trust heuristics or existing confidence in AI assistance, including
comparison with prior knowledge or close reading.  The common design
implication is visible support.  The interface should distinguish bibliographic
identity, accessible supporting material, and the outcome of an automated
source check as separate states.

Traceability contains several reader-visible questions.  The first is source
identity: the interface must identify the paper and revision to which a
summary or answer refers.  The second is source access: the reader needs a
working route to the original paper or to the material retained by the system.
The third is local support: the displayed passage must address the particular
statement being checked.  An automated source check adds another useful
signal, provided that its meaning is stated clearly.  Treating these questions
separately prevents a valid paper link from being presented as proof that every
sentence in a generated response is supported.

These distinctions determine the order of interaction.  A concise summary or
answer appears first, followed by the location and excerpt used as evidence.
The reader can then open the source when the claim matters to the current task.
If the available material is incomplete, the interface reports insufficient
evidence and keeps the original paper action available.  The same sequence
works on a small screen because detailed evidence is disclosed after the paper
and claim have already been identified.

The Wishes group contains only two observation notes and therefore receives
less weight than the larger clusters.  Both notes ask for a durable reading or
knowledge system.  This request concerns continuity across repeated reading
sessions.  It supports the direction already established by the pain-point and
current-solution groups, while the small number of notes does not justify a
separate prevalence claim.  In the resulting requirements, durability is
expressed through stable paper identity, retained reading context, and a
visible path back to evidence.

\subsection{Customer Profile}

The synthesized customer profile is a student or researcher who follows a
changing body of literature and already uses several discovery and storage
tools.  The user alternates between short triage sessions and deeper reading.
The central goal is to maintain a defensible working understanding of a topic
while new papers appear and earlier papers become difficult to recover.  The
profile prioritizes relevant suggestions and traceable evidence.  It also
requires low setup cost and compatibility with existing sources.  Because the
proposed service adapts recommendations over time, the design also provides a
way to correct inaccurate personalization.  The correction mechanism was
introduced as a product safeguard.

Four participant-level counts help define the profile without turning it into
a population estimate.  Eight of ten participants reported difficulty
recovering a paper they had already encountered.  Seven reported difficulty
keeping pace with paper volume, and seven reported weak support for maintaining
links among papers and developing ideas.  Three described storage systems that
became disorganized over time.  These counts were synthesized from the
participant-coded observations and are used only to rank design attention
within this sample.

The profile also contains two reading modes.  In a short session, the user
needs enough information to decide whether a paper deserves later attention.
This mode favors a small set of candidates, visible reasons, abstract-level
context, and one clear action.  In a longer session, the user needs structured
method and result information, source access, question answering, and
connections to related work.  A single dense screen would serve both modes
poorly.  The design therefore moves from briefing card to paper detail and
opens Q\&A and the graph from an already identified paper.

The profile assumes prior tools remain part of the workflow.
Canonical arXiv links let the user return to the original source, while stable
paper identity should preserve the same object across screens.  Source actions
should remain close to generated text, and cached content should identify its
origin.  These choices reduce the amount of trust the user must place in
invisible system state.

The value proposition is summarized as \emph{read, remember, and plan with
evidence}.  A bounded briefing supports triage.  Paper detail and question
answering retain a path to source material.  Explicit interests and documented
reading interactions can inform subsequent recommendations, subject to user
correction.  A limited citation neighborhood helps the reader move between
related papers while remaining legible on a mobile screen.

Figure~\ref{fig:value-proposition} illustrates the workflow synthesized from
the interviews.  It follows a researcher from paper overload to a bounded
briefing, source inspection, connection with related work, and later use of the
recovered evidence.  The storyboard describes the intended reading experience.
System effectiveness is evaluated separately in Chapter~4.

The three verbs describe a repeated process.  \emph{Read} covers initial
triage, structured paper detail, and access to evidence.  \emph{Remember}
covers stable paper identity, retained reading context, and explicit interests.
\emph{Plan} covers the next briefing and the related-paper neighborhood.
Updates influence subsequent briefings, while the reason shown for an earlier
recommendation remains unchanged.

from the interviews: controlling paper volume, recovering prior work, checking
generated content, and exploring related papers.  Personalization control and
mobile continuity are product safeguards introduced by the adaptive and mobile
design.

Several requirements apply across all six outcomes.  Claim-bearing artifacts
need enough provenance to identify their source document.  Behavioral data
require data minimization because reading history can reveal research interests
and professional activity.  Operational uncertainty should appear through
specific states, such as missing evidence, cached content, or a retryable
failure.  These principles apply to the briefing, paper detail, question
answering, and graph views.

Privacy and user control apply across the requirements.  A reading history can
reveal unpublished directions, course work, collaborators, and professional
priorities.  The design therefore limits the reading data collected for
personalization, keeps explicit interests editable, and presents recommendation
reasons in terms a reader can inspect.  Data export, deletion, retention, and
multi-user authorization require further study and are outside the present
customer research.

Evidence communication is another cross-cutting requirement.  The interface
must communicate uncertainty without relying on color alone.  Live and cached
content need textual labels, while processing, incomplete evidence, and failure
need distinct messages and actions.  A reader should be able to tell when an
explanation is supported by accessible source material and when further
checking is required.

The mobile interface must also support accessibility and bounded attention.
Core actions need a text label and a stable touch target.  Briefing cards should
present one primary action, while source and graph actions appear after the
paper identity is established.  Briefings and graph neighborhoods are bounded
so that the interface remains legible on a small screen.  Assistive-technology
testing with participants remains future work.

These requirements define the intended experience and its safety boundaries.
They do not establish implementation or user benefit.  Chapter~3 explains the
architecture, interfaces, and controls selected to satisfy them.
Chapter~4 reports the corresponding software and performance evidence.  The
five-participant prototype study used to refine the interaction design is
presented under Usability Testing in Chapter~3.

\section{Competitor Analysis}

The competitor analysis covers scholarly search services, AI paper assistants,
reference managers, citation-graph tools, and general chat systems.  Five
capability dimensions describe their roles in a research workflow: full-text
input, evidence support, persistent context, proactive delivery, and mobile
form.  Product capabilities were recorded at the time of analysis and may
change as the services evolve.

The dimensions correspond to transitions in the customer workflow.  Full-text
input concerns the material available for reading and question answering after
a paper has been identified.  Evidence support concerns the path from a
generated statement to material that a reader can inspect.  Persistent context
concerns paper collections, notes, preferences, or conversations that survive
the current task.  Proactive delivery concerns new material presented without a
fresh search.  Mobile form distinguishes a dedicated small-screen interaction
from a desktop-oriented web workspace.  These dimensions describe workflow
coverage.  Overall product quality, language fluency, corpus size, and
subscription value lie outside the comparison.

The categories overlap.  A search service may include a library and an AI
answer, while a reference manager may include a reader, synchronization, and
notes.  Table~\ref{tab:competitor-categories} therefore records the main role
of each system and the scope in which context is retained.  Terms such as
``source-linked'' describe visible access to supporting material; they do not
establish that every generated claim is correct.  Claim correctness requires a
separate evaluation of the answer and its cited evidence.

Search and discovery services provide the broadest entry point.  Google Scholar
supports relevance- and date-oriented search, links to available full text,
related-paper and cited-by navigation, citation export, and email alerts for
queries, authors, and citations \cite{googleScholarHelp2026}.  arXiv has a
different role: it hosts author-submitted research articles and makes them
openly accessible \cite{arxivAnnual2023}.
Semantic Scholar extends discovery with a personal library, folder-based
Research Feeds, alerts, short paper summaries, and limited paper question
answering with supporting statements \cite{semanticScholarProduct2026}.  These
capabilities preserve more state than a single result page.  Their persistent
objects remain search queries, library folders, alerts, and feeds.  A later
reading session may still require the reader to re-establish how a generated
explanation, a saved paper, and a related-paper view belong to the same task.

AI literature assistants cover more of the interpretation stage.  Elicit
offers semantic paper search, question-specific abstract summaries,
configurable extraction columns, filtering, and export to formats used by
reference managers \cite{elicitHelp2026}.  Consensus combines academic search
with citation-backed synthesis and full-text question answering for selected
papers or collections \cite{consensusHelp2026}.  SciSpace provides search,
paper organization, PDF question answering, and citation export
\cite{scispaceHelp2026}.  NotebookLM answers over a selected set of uploaded
sources and exposes quotations that navigate back to their source context
\cite{notebookLMHelp2026}.  Visible evidence is therefore an established
capability in this category.  The design question for MNEME concerns continuity:
whether the same paper identity, recommendation reason, evidence state, and
reading action remain connected when the reader moves from a briefing into
paper detail, question answering, and related work.

Reference managers provide the strongest baseline for durable personal
collections.  Zotero synchronizes item metadata, notes, tags, links, and
optional attachment files across devices \cite{zoteroSync2026}.  Its PDF reader
connects highlights and notes to citations and can return the reader to the
annotated page \cite{zoteroDocs2026}.  These capabilities directly support
long-term retention and formal academic writing.  The interviews show that
later recovery also depends on the quality of the maintained structure.  That
structure can become inconsistent, and the remembered cue may be an idea rather
than a title, author, folder, or tag.  MNEME complements this role with a
bounded incoming briefing, a stored reason for each recommendation, and a path
from generated content to the paper.  Bibliography preparation and document
citation remain within reference-manager workflows.

Citation-graph tools address exploration around a known paper.  Connected
Papers constructs a force-directed similarity graph from co-citation and
bibliographic coupling.  Nearby nodes can therefore be topically related even
when one paper does not directly cite the other \cite{connectedPapersAbout2026}.
This distinction matters for interpretation: visual proximity can represent a
similarity measure, a direct citation, or another modeled relation depending on
the tool.  MNEME uses a bounded neighborhood whose edges are labelled for the
relationship exposed by the backend.  The mobile view retains a selected node
and a direct paper action so that exploration returns to reading without losing
the current graph context.

General chat systems provide broad language interaction and can accept pasted,
uploaded, or retrieved sources.  Their source controls, workspace persistence,
and proactive functions vary by product and subscription.  The comparison
therefore treats these capabilities as tool dependent.  For a literature
workflow, a general conversation also leaves several choices to the user: which
paper revision is in scope, which source supports a statement, which reading
state should persist, and how a related paper becomes part of a later briefing.
MNEME reduces this flexibility by beginning from a paper object with a canonical
identifier and by limiting question answering to the evidence associated with
that paper.

The main gap appears at the handoffs between capable tools.  A researcher can
discover a paper through Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar, save it to a
reference manager, upload it to an AI assistant, and open a separate graph
around it.  Each stage can succeed while the researcher repeatedly reconstructs
paper identity, purpose, evidence, and personal context.  The interview results
show why these handoffs matter: participants used several effective tools and
still reported lost papers, deteriorating organization, and manual source
checking.  A combined workflow must preserve the state carried across the
handoffs.  Placing the existing functions on one screen alone would leave this
problem unresolved.

This comparison leads to six concrete design consequences.  First, canonical
paper identity must remain stable across discovery, briefing, detail, question
answering, local storage, and graph views.  Second, the briefing must remain
small enough for triage and must record an intelligible recommendation reason.
Third, generated text must expose accessible supporting material and an
explicit insufficient-evidence state.  Fourth, persistent personalization must
remain correctable by the reader.  Fifth, graph exploration must be bounded and
must preserve the selected paper and its relationship.  Sixth, the mobile
application must distinguish live, cached, processing, fallback, and failure
states.  These consequences correspond to the customer requirements in
Table~\ref{tab:customer-requirements} and define the design developed in
Chapter~3.

The MNEME row in Table~\ref{tab:competitor-categories} summarizes this design
scope.  MNEME accepts a narrower role than a general search engine, a complete
reference manager, or an unrestricted chat system.  Its briefing is bounded,
its question answering is paper-specific, and its graph is limited for mobile
interaction.  The intended benefit is continuity across those stages.
Controlled comparisons of search coverage, language quality, bibliography
management, or overall research productivity would require separate
head-to-head studies.  Chapter~4 evaluates the implemented MNEME workflow
within its stated scope.
