\chapter{Design Specification}

This chapter develops MNEME's design specification from the customer research
and competitor study completed before application development.  Ten
semi-structured interviews were organized through an affinity map and then
synthesized into a customer profile.  The same evidence was compared with
existing research tools to identify the product gap.  The chapter concludes
with the resulting value proposition and storyboard.

\section{Customer Requirements}

The customer study examined the complete path from discovering a paper to
remembering, verifying, and connecting it later.  It focused on current
research practice before presenting MNEME as a possible solution.  This
sequence allowed the requirements to grow from reported behavior and concrete
problems rather than from reactions to a proposed feature set.

\subsection{User Interview Questionnaire}

The questionnaire targeted students and researchers who regularly follow a
changing academic literature.  Participants came from machine learning and
natural language processing, computer vision, and program verification, with
seniority ranging from first-year master's students to a lecturer.  This range
was selected to examine whether the same workflow problems appeared across
fields with different publication speeds and reading practices.

The questionnaire was designed around recent behavior.  Participants were not
shown the proposed application before the interview, and the questions avoided
assuming that a particular difficulty or solution already existed.  Open
questions invited narrative answers, while prompts such as ``What happened
next?'', ``What made that difficult?'', and ``How did it affect your work?''
encouraged participants to describe the goal and consequence of each episode.

The eleven questions followed the order of a typical literature workflow.
Q1--Q3 established the participant's field, reading frequency, and current
tools.  Q4--Q7 examined how papers were discovered and selected when the number
of candidates became difficult to manage.  Q8 and Q9 asked how papers were
stored and later recovered.  Q10 concerned links among papers, concepts,
authors, and research directions.  Q11 asked what would make an AI-generated
summary or answer trustworthy.  The complete questionnaire and follow-up
prompts appear in Appendix~\ref{app:interview}.

This structure served three design purposes.  The discovery and selection
questions identified the information needed for rapid triage.  The storage and
recall questions showed what context must survive after a reading session.
The connection and trust questions established the need for related-paper
navigation and visible source support.

The order of the questions also reduced the risk that later discussion would
change the meaning of earlier answers.  General questions about research area
and existing tools came first, followed by requests for a specific discovery
or recovery episode.  Only the final question mentioned AI-generated
assistance.  As a result, reports of lost papers, manual source checking, and
fragmented storage arose from the participant's own account of current work.

The questionnaire remained consistent across interviews while allowing
follow-up questions when a participant described an unfamiliar practice.  The
same core questions made the observations comparable, and the open format
preserved differences between fast-moving preprint fields and
proof-oriented research areas.  The instrument was therefore used to discover
requirements and their context, rather than to ask participants to approve a
preselected design.

\subsection{Customer Interviews}

Ten semi-structured interviews were conducted and identified as P1--P10.
Participants were recruited through research laboratories, paper-reading
groups, and campus study areas.  Interviews took place individually in person
or by video call and lasted approximately fifteen minutes on average.
Interview notes were converted into single-observation statements after each
session and retained with the corresponding participant code for affinity
mapping.

Participants used a mixture of discovery and storage tools.  Google Scholar
appeared in seven participant records and arXiv in six; four participants also
received papers from an advisor or another researcher.  Mendeley, Zotero,
folders, spreadsheets, bookmarks, and personal note pages were all used for
storage.  The interviews therefore described a workflow distributed across
several tools rather than one complete environment.

The mixture of tools clarifies where effort is repeated.  Search engines help
participants find candidates, reference managers preserve bibliographic
records, folders hold documents, and note systems retain personal
interpretation.  A paper can exist in all four places while its purpose and
relationship to the current research question remain only in the reader's
memory.  Participants often recovered a paper by remembering a phrase, figure,
or idea rather than a title, author, folder, or tag.

Paper selection began with the paper itself.  Seven participant records
mentioned reading the abstract, five mentioned figures or tables, four
mentioned venue reputation, and three mentioned the availability of code.
These preferences indicate that an initial paper view should present enough
information for triage without forcing the reader into a full document or a
long generated account.

Publication culture affected the volume and style of reading, although it did
not remove the downstream problems.  Machine-learning and computer-vision
participants described rapidly changing preprint streams, whereas program
verification involved slower and more proof-oriented reading.  Participants
from all three areas still described later recovery, personal organization,
and source-checking difficulties.  The common requirement is therefore to
preserve useful context after discovery while allowing the selected papers and
reading pace to vary by field.

The strongest recurring problems appeared after discovery.  Participants
described losing papers whose titles or authors they no longer remembered,
accumulating more papers than they could read, and keeping relationships among
papers only in memory.  They also described checking AI-generated explanations
against the original paper when the result mattered to their work.  These
observations motivated requirements for a bounded briefing, stable paper
identity, source-linked assistance, persistent research context, and a direct
route between related papers.

The study provides formative evidence from ten participants in
computing-related fields.  The counts in this chapter describe the recorded
sample and were used to prioritize the design; they are not population
estimates.  Interview notes were reorganized as observation statements rather
than analyzed from full transcripts, so the affinity map records the design
evidence available from this study.

\subsection{Affinity Map}

Each interview observation was placed on a digital note and tagged with its
participant code.  The notes were clustered bottom-up into subthemes and then
into four top-level groups: Pain Points, Current Solutions, Trust in AI, and
Wishes.  Green identifies a top-level group, pink and blue identify successive
clusters, and yellow boxes contain observation statements.  The number in a
group box is the number of coded notes in that group, while the number attached
to an observation records its tally.  A participant could contribute more than
one observation, so note counts and participant counts have different
meanings.

The Pain Points group contains 36 notes.  Information overload accounts for 12
notes, difficulty recovering a previously encountered paper for 10, missing
links among papers and ideas for 9, and storage deterioration for 5.
Figures~\ref{fig:affinity-pain-overload} and
\ref{fig:affinity-pain-continuity} show these four clusters.  The observations
include deadline periods that produced tens or hundreds of candidates in a
week, searches lasting from twenty minutes to one hour for a known paper, and
folder or tag systems that became difficult to use over time.

The four pain clusters describe related but different failures.  Information
overload affects the decision about what to read now.  Recovery failure occurs
later, when the researcher remembers an idea but cannot reconstruct enough
paper identity to locate it.  Storage deterioration concerns the systems that
hold files and records, while missing links concern the relationships among
papers and developing ideas.  A folder may retain a PDF and still fail to help
the researcher recall why it was saved or how it relates to another paper.

The participant codes and tallies keep these interpretations tied to the
recorded observations.  Four participant records describe conference or
deadline periods that produce tens or hundreds of candidate papers in a week.
Two describe spending between twenty minutes and one hour recovering a paper
that had already been encountered, and three report that they do not maintain
a citation or concept map of their own.  These examples connect the clustered
themes to concrete episodes in the interviews.

The Current Solutions group contains 74 notes and shows why the workflow
remains fragmented.  Discovery channels account for 28 notes, management and
note tools for 13, and paper-selection signals for 33.  Search services,
reference managers, folders, and note systems each solve a part of the task,
but moving among them requires the researcher to reconstruct the paper's
identity, purpose, and relationship to earlier work.

The distribution within Current Solutions also indicates what a new product
should preserve.  Google Scholar and arXiv already provide familiar discovery
routes, while Mendeley, Zotero, folders, and spreadsheets already hold valued
collections.  The design opportunity lies between these tools: carrying a
bounded recommendation into reading, keeping the reason for that
recommendation, and returning from an explanation or related-paper view to the
same paper record.  This interpretation avoids treating established discovery
and reference-management tools as functions that must be rebuilt.

The Trust in AI group contains 21 notes.  Thirteen concern traceability: five
participant records ask for the exact section, figure, or page, three express
distrust of summaries without a source pointer, and two emphasize checking the
original paper.  The remaining notes describe other trust heuristics or prior
positive use of AI assistance.  Figure~\ref{fig:affinity-trust} shows the two
groups.  The design consequence is that a summary or answer should retain a
visible path to the source material used to produce it.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/chapter2_affinity_trust.png}
  \caption{Affinity-map observations concerning trust in AI-generated
  summaries and answers.}
  \label{fig:affinity-trust}
\end{figure}

Traceability contains two distinct requirements.  The first is source
identity: the user must know which paper a summary or answer describes.  The
second is local support: the interface should expose the passage, section, or
figure associated with the statement being inspected.  A paper link alone
solves the first requirement but leaves the user to search the document again.
The interview observations therefore support placing evidence access close to
generated content.

The Wishes group contains two notes asking for a durable reading or knowledge
system.  Although this group is small, its direction agrees with the larger
pain-point and current-solution groups.  Taken together, the map shifts the
design emphasis from finding more papers to retaining, retrieving, connecting,
and checking papers that have already entered the researcher's workflow.

\subsection{Customer Profile}

The synthesized customer is a student or researcher who follows a changing
body of literature and already uses several discovery and storage tools.  The
user alternates between short triage sessions and deeper reading.  The primary
goal is to maintain a useful understanding of a topic while new papers appear
and earlier papers become difficult to recover.  The profile therefore
prioritizes relevant suggestions, low setup cost, stable paper identity, and
evidence that can be checked against the source.

Four participant-level findings define the main design priorities.  Eight of
ten participants reported difficulty recovering a paper they had already
encountered.  Seven reported difficulty keeping pace with paper volume, and
seven reported weak support for maintaining links among papers and ideas.
Three described storage systems that became disorganized over time.  These
participant-level counts are consistent with the note-level affinity clusters:
the profile is primarily shaped by recovery, volume, and connection problems,
with storage deterioration as a smaller but concrete concern.

Figure~\ref{fig:customer-pain-points} presents these four findings together
with the interview notes from which they were derived.  The counts establish
paper recovery, reading volume, and paper--idea connections as the leading
requirements for the design.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter2_customer_profile-05.png}
  \caption{Principal workflow pain points and supporting interview evidence.}
  \label{fig:customer-pain-points}

The profile also records the practices that a new tool must respect.
Participants already discover papers through Google Scholar, arXiv, and human
referrals; they triage by abstract, figures, venue, and code availability; and
they store papers in tools that remain part of their research process.  The
application should therefore preserve canonical paper links and bibliographic
identity rather than attempting to replace these established sources.

Two reading modes follow from this profile.  During a short session, the user
needs enough information to decide whether a paper deserves attention: a small
number of candidates, recognizable metadata, a concise description, and a
clear reason for selection.  During deeper reading, the user needs method and
result information, source access, question answering, and links to related
work.  Separating these modes avoids placing the entire research workflow on
one dense screen.

The requirements form a continuous path.  A bounded briefing addresses volume
at the point of entry.  Stable identity and retained context support later
recovery.  Source access supports verification during reading, while a
related-paper view externalizes connections that participants otherwise kept
in memory.  Editable interests keep this repeated process aligned with the
reader's changing research direction.  The competitor analysis examines how
existing products cover these needs separately and where the handoffs remain.

\section{Competitor Analysis}

The competitor analysis examines the tools that occupy different stages of
the same research workflow: scholarly search, AI-assisted reading, reference
management, citation exploration, and mobile discovery.  The comparison asks
which stages each product supports and what context the researcher must still
carry manually between them.  The comparison uses five workflow dimensions:
candidate discovery, assistance during reading, inspectable evidence,
persistent research context, and movement to related work.  These dimensions
come directly from the customer requirements rather than from a general
ranking of product quality.

Figure~\ref{fig:competitor-matrix} compares five representative products and
MNEME across nine features derived from the customer requirements.  A check
mark indicates direct support, a cross indicates that the feature is absent,
and a tilde indicates partial support.  The final column states the intended
coverage of the MNEME design.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter2_competitor-14.png}
  \caption{Competitive feature matrix used to define MNEME's product
  position.}
  \label{fig:competitor-matrix}
\end{figure}

Search services provide the broadest entry point.  Google Scholar supports
relevance- and date-oriented search, access to available full text, cited-by
and related-paper navigation, citation export, and alerts
\cite{googleScholarHelp2026}.  arXiv provides open access to author-submitted
research articles \cite{arxivAnnual2023}.  Semantic Scholar adds a personal
library, Research Feeds, alerts, short paper summaries, and limited
paper-question answering with supporting statements
\cite{semanticScholarProduct2026}.  These services are strong at discovery,
but the researcher's reading purpose and later interpretation still have to be
maintained across subsequent tools.

The persistent objects in this family are commonly queries, alerts, library
folders, and feeds.  They are valuable because they let a researcher return to
results without repeating the same search.  The interview findings point to a
different form of persistence as well: remembering why one paper mattered,
what evidence was inspected, and how it connected with a developing research
direction.  MNEME treats the search result as the beginning of this process
rather than its final state.

AI literature assistants support interpretation and synthesis.  Elicit offers
semantic search, question-specific abstract summaries, configurable extraction
columns, filtering, and export \cite{elicitHelp2026}.  Consensus provides
citation-backed synthesis and question answering over selected papers
\cite{consensusHelp2026}.  SciSpace combines paper search, organization, PDF
question answering, and citation export \cite{scispaceHelp2026}, while
NotebookLM answers over a user-selected source collection and links quotations
back to their context \cite{notebookLMHelp2026}.  These products reduce work
inside a reading task, but the researcher still decides which papers enter the
workspace and how the result reconnects with later reading.

Evidence support also differs among assistants.  Some products display
citations or quotations next to generated text, while others primarily provide
the document or result list from which an answer was produced.  The customer
study does not require one universal presentation, but it does require a short
path from an important statement to material that can be inspected.  This
requirement influences the placement of source actions in the later application
design.

Reference managers concentrate on persistent collections.  Zotero synchronizes
item metadata, notes, tags, links, and optional attachments
\cite{zoteroSync2026}; its reader connects annotations with citations and can
return to the annotated page \cite{zoteroDocs2026}.  Citation-graph tools serve
a different purpose.  Connected Papers, for example, places papers by modeled
similarity derived from co-citation and bibliographic coupling rather than by
direct citation alone \cite{connectedPapersAbout2026}.  These tools support
collection and exploration, yet neither function alone closes the path from a
new recommendation to reading, verification, and later recall.

Mobile discovery products contribute convenient access and proactive feeds,
which directly address the short-session reading mode identified in the
customer profile.  Their role demonstrates that a mobile interface can support
paper triage without reproducing a desktop workspace.  The remaining design
question is how the same small-screen flow can continue into evidence
inspection, retained interests, and related-paper navigation.

The comparison identifies a gap at the handoffs between capable products.  A
researcher can discover a paper in a search service, save it to a reference
manager, upload it to an AI assistant, and open a separate graph around it.
Each tool can complete its own task while the researcher repeatedly
reconstructs why the paper matters, which source supports a generated
statement, and how the paper relates to earlier work.  MNEME therefore focuses
on preserving this context across a bounded briefing, paper reading,
source-linked assistance, retained interests, and related-paper exploration.

This position complements the products in
Figure~\ref{fig:competitor-matrix}.
Search engines remain appropriate for broad exploration, reference managers
remain appropriate for bibliography and collection maintenance, and
specialized assistants remain useful for extraction or synthesis.  MNEME
concentrates on the continuity of one reading process: a reasoned incoming
selection, an identifiable paper, inspectable assistance, retained research
context, and a clear route to related work.  These elements form the basis of
the value proposition.

\section{Value Proposition}

The customer research and competitor analysis lead to the value proposition
\emph{Read, Remember, Plan}.  MNEME is a mobile-native research companion that
reads papers through concise, source-linked assistance, remembers papers and
research interests across sessions, and plans what the researcher should read
or revisit next.  The three parts correspond directly to the identified
problems of paper volume, later recovery, weak links among papers and ideas,
and trust in generated explanations.

Existing tools already help researchers search, store documents, and analyze a
selected set of papers.  The value proposition connects these activities into
a durable reading process.  It keeps the source of generated material
inspectable, makes previously read papers easier to recognize and revisit, and
uses retained context to guide the next small set of papers.  The product is
therefore organized around repeated engagement with literature rather than a
single query or one document upload.

\emph{Read} covers the transition from a bounded set of candidate papers to
triage and deeper examination.  A briefing provides enough metadata, summary
context, and recommendation rationale to support the initial decision.  Once a
paper is opened, structured detail and source-linked assistance support
understanding without separating the generated account from the paper to which
it refers.  This part of the proposition addresses paper volume and the
interview preference for abstract- and figure-led triage.

\emph{Remember} covers stable paper identity, retained reading context, and
research interests that survive the current session.  It responds to the
dominant recovery problem in the customer profile: the user may remember an
idea or figure while forgetting the title, author, or folder.  Preserving the
paper, the reason it was selected, and its surrounding context creates more
retrieval cues than a filename or bookmark alone.

\emph{Plan} uses the retained context to prepare a later briefing and to move
from the current paper into a related-paper neighborhood.  It addresses the
volume problem by limiting what arrives at one time and the connection problem
by making nearby work visible from an already identified paper.  The user can
correct explicit interests, so the next briefing follows a changing research
direction rather than treating every past interaction as permanent.

The three verbs describe one loop.  Reading produces paper-specific context;
remembering preserves the parts that matter beyond the session; planning uses
that context to select or reconnect the next reading action.  This loop is the
direct synthesis of the customer requirements and competitor analysis, and it
provides the narrative for the storyboard.

\paragraph{Storyboard.}
The storyboard in Figure~\ref{fig:storyboard} expresses the same value
proposition as one research episode.  A graduate student begins with more
papers than she can manage, receives a limited briefing, and opens a paper
account with source links.  She then places the paper in a connected research
context and later returns to the relevant paper and evidence during a group
meeting.  The sequence joins the customer problems, proposed capabilities,
and intended outcome in a form that can guide the application design.

The first frame establishes the volume problem in an ordinary research
setting.  The second and third frames show the transition from a paced
selection to a paper account whose claims can be checked.  The fourth frame
externalizes the connection between the paper and related research, while the
final frame shows why retention matters: the reader can recover both the paper
and the evidence when the work is discussed later.  The storyboard therefore
summarizes the intended experience and provides a reference for the application
flow developed in Chapter~3.

The design specification therefore ends with a single product direction:
support the reader after a paper has been found by keeping paper identity,
evidence, interests, and related work connected throughout the reading
process.
